Playbook 01 · Power BI to Fabric

Validate, deploy and go live

This is the gate between development and business value. It proves the new estate is accurate, fast and secure; wins the business's written acceptance; releases through the same path every time; runs old and new side by side; and then retires the legacy estate in a way that can be undone until the last step.

  • Phase III · Validate and release
  • Audience QA engineers, business testers, release managers, platform admins
  • Output signed-off release, live production, retired legacy estate

The release path and its gates

Treat the path from build to retirement as a sequence of gates. Each gate has an approver and a recorded decision, and nothing passes because a date arrived.

Quality assurance

Before any asset reaches a business user, the technical team validates it on four pillars: data accuracy, functional correctness, performance and security. Record the result per asset on a checklist, so the matrix row can show "QA passed" with evidence.

Data reconciliation

Reconciliation is the most important and most time-consuming test. It shows that the numbers in the new estate equal the numbers in the legacy estate for the same business question. A difference in a key metric can come from anywhere in the chain: ingestion, a Silver rule, a Gold aggregate, or the DAX measure. So reconcile in layers, from the cheapest broad check to the most specific one, and stop at the first layer where numbers diverge.

Start with aggregate checks (total revenue, total customers), then drill into dimensional ones (revenue by category and region). For large estates, automate the comparison. A layer-by-layer control-total notebook is a good first automation:

from pyspark.sql import functions as F

bronze = (spark.read.format("delta").load(bronze_path)               # path as in the Silver notebook
          .withColumn("net_amount", F.col("Amount").cast("decimal(18,2)")))
silver = spark.read.table("orders")
quarantine = spark.read.table("orders_quarantine")

# Rows removed by a documented rule (for example, test accounts).
excluded = bronze.filter(F.upper(F.trim("CustomerCode")).startswith("TEST"))

def totals(df, layer):
    return df.agg(F.count("*").alias("row_count"),
                  F.sum("net_amount").alias("total")).withColumn("layer", F.lit(layer))

result = (totals(bronze, "bronze")
          .unionByName(totals(silver, "silver"))
          .unionByName(totals(quarantine, "quarantine"))
          .unionByName(totals(excluded, "excluded")))
result.show()
# Expect: bronze rows = silver + quarantine + excluded, and the same for the totals.

User acceptance testing

UAT is where business users confirm the solution fits their work. It is as much about building confidence as finding defects, and it produces the business sign-off that unlocks production.

  • Representative testers. Choose them with business leaders: power users who will scrutinize details, and occasional consumers who judge clarity and usability.
  • Environment. The TST workspaces, loaded with production-like volumes so performance and results are realistic. Where the data is sensitive, apply the same controls as production, or mask it, in line with sensitivity labels and DLP. Give testers the same roles and licenses that real users will have, or the test proves nothing about access.
  • Scripts, not "have a look". Write scenarios that follow real work: a task to perform and an outcome to verify against something the tester already knows.
  • One log. Record every defect, question and change request in a single tracker, with a regular triage meeting to prioritize and assign.
  • Sign-off. UAT ends only when all Critical and High issues are resolved or explicitly accepted, and the named business stakeholders give written sign-off. It is a gate, not a courtesy.

Production deployment

Production deployment should be the execution of a rehearsed process, not an event. Rehearse it at least once into TST, with the same pipeline, rules and variables that production will use. The release mechanisms, Git workflow and automation options are described in environments and CI/CD; the dedicated playbooks cover deployment, variable libraries, and the deployment checklist.

  1. Compare and promote. In the deployment pipeline, compare TST and PRD and deploy only the validated items: lakehouses, warehouses, notebooks, pipelines, semantic models, reports. Review the comparison; unexpected differences are a stop signal.
  2. Apply and verify rules. Deployment rules, and variable libraries where used, repoint connections and parameters for production. Rules are supported for specific item types only, so check each item that must change, and confirm every connection points to production endpoints.
  3. Enable the schedule. Configure and enable the schedule of the hub pipeline, and confirm the alert path with a forced failure.
  4. Publish the apps. Set audiences and navigation and publish or update the organizational apps (see organizational apps).
  5. Validate security. Confirm workspace roles, app audiences, model roles and sensitivity labels with real viewer accounts.
  6. Run the first production load and replay the reconciliation set against production.
  • Production capacity is assigned, sized and monitored; the Capacity Metrics app is connected.
  • Service principals or workspace identities have the permissions they need, and no more.
  • Refresh and framing behavior is configured for every model (schedule, automatic updates or pipeline step).
  • A rollback plan exists: during the parallel run the legacy reports are still live, so rollback means pointing users back and pausing the new schedule while the defect is fixed.
  • Support contacts, escalation and the communication plan are ready (see support).

In mature setups a merge to the main branch triggers the same deployment through the Fabric APIs, for example with the pipeline shown on the architecture page. The principle does not change with the automation level: the same process, the same checks, every time.

Go-live and parallel run

Go-live starts with the communication plan: a formal announcement with direct links to the new apps, the training on offer and the support route. Then run the new and legacy estates in parallel for a defined period, typically two to four weeks.

  • Why parallel. It gives users a safety net while they adapt, and gives the team a last window to reconcile on live production data.
  • Hypercare. Heightened support availability during the period: faster response, a visible channel, daily triage of issues in the first days.
  • Measure adoption. Track usage of the new reports against the legacy ones with the activity events you have been exporting. A report that nobody opens is not yet migrated.
  • Mind the cost. Both estates consume capacity. If the legacy content was reassigned to the F SKU, its cost is the same capacity; if it still sits on a retiring P SKU, plan the end of that term (see the licensing section).
  • Exit criteria. Define them before the run starts: reconciliation within tolerance on live data, critical issues closed, adoption at an agreed level, and no unresolved objections on the exception register.

Decommission the legacy estate

Retiring the old estate is the step most often skipped, and skipping it leaves two sources of truth, user confusion, wasted capacity and ongoing technical debt. Use a protocol whose first steps are fully reversible and whose last step is not.

  1. Confirm there is nothing left to lose. Check the activity data for recent use, and the exception register and the matrix for assets marked Retire. Notify owners again before anything changes.
  2. Mark and restrict. Rename the legacy workspaces and reports with a clear prefix such as RETIRED_, and remove user access, leaving only administrators. Messages from users at this point are useful signals, not failures.
  3. Halt refreshes. Disable the schedules for every legacy model and dataflow so they stop consuming capacity and the data goes stale. Also stop subscriptions, data alerts and gateway data sources that only the legacy content used.
  4. Hold for a grace period (for example, three months), with the assets preserved but inaccessible.
  5. Archive. Store what you may need later in secure storage: downloadable PBIX files where the service allows it, and model and report definitions (TMDL and PBIR) where it does not, plus the specification package. Not every model can be downloaded as a PBIX (for example, models with incremental refresh), so see the restrictions.
  6. Delete. After the sponsor's approval, delete the workspaces and content, retire legacy service principals and unused gateway connections, and record the outcome in the matrix. Release or reassign the old capacity as the licensing plan says.

Workspace recovery is a safety net, not an archive

A deleted workspace can be restored by a tenant administrator during a retention period (seven days by default, configurable from 7 to 90 days), and personal workspaces have a fixed 30 days. A restored workspace comes back without its workspace identity. Do not plan to rely on this: archive before you delete.

Verify against Microsoft Learn

Retention periods and restore steps are in Set up and manage workspace retention and Retention and recovery in Fabric. Confirm the values in your tenant before you design the grace period around them.

Key takeaways

  • Four gates with named approvers: QA, business sign-off, go-live and retirement.
  • Reconcile in layers, from source to business view, and stop at the first rung that diverges. Agree tolerances up front.
  • Test for Direct Lake fallback, capacity impact and security as a viewer, not only for correct numbers.
  • Deploy by the rehearsed pipeline; verify rules and variables after promotion; have a rollback plan.
  • Run in parallel with defined exit criteria, then retire in reversible steps, archive first, delete last.
