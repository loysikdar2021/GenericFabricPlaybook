Playbook 01 · Power BI to Fabric

Change, support and project plan

A migration succeeds when people use the new platform and trust it, not when the last pipeline runs. This page covers communication and training, the support and optimization model that follows go-live, and a project plan template you can adapt: a phased task table and a timeline in relative weeks, with no calendar dates.

Change management

People adopt a platform when they understand why it is changing, see the benefit for their own work, and feel supported while they learn. Plan this from the start of the project, not from the week before go-live. The plan has two halves: a communication plan that says what to tell whom, and a role-based training curriculum that gives each group what it needs.

Name a champion in every business area

A small network of champions, one per business area, multiplies the reach of the central team. They hear the objections first, test the scripts in UAT, and answer the "where did my report go?" questions in their own teams.

Role-based training

One course for everyone does not work. Tailor it to the persona and deliver it through a mix of live workshops, on-demand material and hands-on labs, both before and after go-live.

For a team coming from Pro-era habits, the largest shift is not a tool but a way of working: commits, pull requests and releases in place of publishing a file from a laptop. Budget time for it, because a team that does not adopt source control undermines the whole CI/CD strategy.

Post-migration support and optimization

Go-live is the start of the platform's operational life, not the end of the project. Move from a project mindset to a product mindset: the analytics platform has an owner, a roadmap, a backlog and a release rhythm.

Support channels

  • A help desk or ticketing system for logging and tracking issues, with categories that match the platform (access, data question, defect, change request) and target response times.
  • Office hours with the BI team, scheduled and published, where users get live help.
  • A community of practice in a dedicated Teams channel or Viva Engage community, for peer help, announcements and sharing.
  • Escalation paths to the platform owner for incidents, and a status page or channel for known issues.

Monitoring adoption, performance and cost

  • User adoption. Active users, report views, the share of views on the new estate versus any legacy remainder, and self-service content created. Use the activity events you started exporting during the assessment, the audit log, and the built-in usage reports. Low adoption points to a training or promotion gap.
  • Capacity and performance. Use the Capacity Metrics app to watch utilization, smoothing, throttling and the items that consume the most. Community accelerators such as Fabric Unified Admin Monitoring (FUAM), published in the Fabric Toolbox repository, extend this with tenant-wide history; they are community-driven and not a supported Microsoft product, so deploy them deliberately and test their own capacity use.
  • Cost. Review spend regularly. Adjust the mix of reserved and pay-as-you-go capacity to actual usage, and use pause and resume where workloads allow, as covered in licensing and cost and the capacity behavior section.
  • Pipeline health. Failure rates, durations and the freshness of each model, from the alerting set up in the orchestration step.

Continuous improvement and retrospective

Keep one prioritized backlog fed by user feedback, tickets and monitoring, and work through it with the same agile and CI/CD practices you used to build the platform. Hold a project retrospective with the core team and key stakeholders: what went well, what to change, and the lessons that apply to every future data initiative. Record them in the same repository as the specification packages, so the next project starts from them.

Project plan template

The template below is a starting point, not a schedule. Tasks marked "per area" repeat for each business area or wave; "once" tasks happen once for the whole program; "case by case" tasks depend on the area. It allows for multiple consumption layers after Gold, as a multi-domain implementation needs.

Plan in waves, not in one pass

A single pass (every extract, then every build, then every test) puts the first thing a business owner can see far into the program. Instead, group the signed-off matrix into waves by business area and value. Wave 1 is a pilot that goes through the whole chain and settles naming, shared code, alerting, the reconciliation method and the release path. Later waves reuse all of it and overlap.

Phase exit checklists and success measures

Use these as the entry criteria for the next phase. If an item cannot be ticked, the phase is not finished.

Phase I exit

  • Capacity is in place and the reassignment runbook has been rehearsed on a non-critical workspace.
  • Architecture, naming, branching and governance decisions are recorded and approved.
  • The rationalization matrix is signed by business and IT leadership; the wave plan exists.
  • A daily activity export is running and the exception register is open.

Phase II exit (per wave)

  • Every rebuilt asset has a specification package and baselines.
  • Bronze, Silver and Gold tables trace to the data-mapping document; rejected rows are quarantined and reviewed.
  • Models are certified candidates, with tested security and a recorded storage-mode decision.
  • The hub pipeline runs the wave end to end and alerts reach a monitored destination.

Phase III exit

  • Reconciliation is within tolerance in production; no Critical or unaccepted High defects.
  • Written business sign-off exists for each wave.
  • The parallel run met its exit criteria; adoption is confirmed from usage data.
  • Legacy content is archived, then deleted, and the matrix reflects the outcome.

Phase IV exit

  • Support channels, owners and response targets are published and in use.
  • Adoption, performance and cost reports run on a regular cadence.
  • A backlog and release rhythm exist; the retrospective is held and its lessons are filed.

Principles to hold throughout

  1. Strategic foundation first. Licensing and cost analysis, a deliberate architecture pattern based on team skills, and governance are prerequisites, not parallel work.
  2. Rationalize, do not just migrate. A data-driven decision per asset is the cheapest scope reduction and the best risk control.
  3. Embrace engineering discipline. Source control and release pipelines make the platform maintainable, and the team needs training to use them.
  4. Decouple and centralize. Certified semantic models feeding thin reports keep one version of the truth.
  5. People drive adoption. Communication and role-based training are as critical as the build.

Key takeaways

  • Plan communication and training from the first week: phased messages per audience and a curriculum per persona, with developers as the most important and most often neglected group.
  • Run the platform as a product: support channels, monitoring of adoption, capacity and cost, one backlog, and a release rhythm.
  • Plan in waves, with a pilot that sets the patterns. Express every date as a relative week, and replace the sample numbers with your own estimate.
  • Use the phase tables and exit checklists as gates: a phase is finished when its checklist can be ticked.
  • Hold a retrospective and file the lessons with the specification packages.
